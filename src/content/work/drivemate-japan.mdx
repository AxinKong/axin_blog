## Context

**DriveMate Japan** is a two-sided platform that matches people in Japan who are nervous
about driving with **vetted coaches and practice partners** nearby — chosen by the learner,
by area, language, speciality and hourly rate.

Live: [drivematejapan.vercel.app](https://drivematejapan.vercel.app) · JP / EN / ZH

## 01. Problem

Japan has a large population of licence holders who have not driven for years. Many are
women who stopped after marriage or childbirth and then need to drive again for school runs
and family logistics. Many are foreign residents for whom language and unfamiliar traffic
norms add a second layer on top of rusty skills.

Their anxieties are specific, and driving schools do not cover them:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| 路上に出るのが怖い | Afraid of driving on real roads |
| 駐車が苦手 | Can't park confidently |
| 子どもを乗せた送迎が不安 | Nervous driving with children in the car |
| 夜間運転が怖い | Afraid of driving at night |
| 高速道路が怖い | Afraid of the expressway |
| レンタカー運転が怖い | Afraid of renting a car on a trip |

These are *post-licence* problems. Driving schools teach you to pass a test; nobody
teaches you to merge onto the Shuto Expressway with two kids in the back.

## 02. The gap

Japan's driving schools assign you an instructor. You don't pick them — and once you have
the licence, the relationship ends. There is no established way to go back and practise with
someone.

So for a new or rusty driver, the thing that would help most is the one thing nobody in Japan
offers: **choosing who teaches you.**

That is the whole opening — not a better booking UX, not cheaper lessons. Just being able to
pick the person: someone who speaks your language, someone you feel at ease with, someone who
teaches the exact thing you are afraid of.

## 03. Three demand segments

The original wedge is trust: a nervous learner wants to be taught by someone she can relax
with. That same wedge opens onto three distinct segments, with different prices and very
different acquisition paths.

| Segment | Who | The sentence they say | Price / 2h |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Paper Driver** | Japanese, licensed, hasn't driven in years | 「久しぶりに運転したい」 | ¥12,000–15,000 |
| **Foreign Driver** | Foreign residents with a Japanese licence | *"I have a licence but I'm not confident driving in Japan."* | ¥15,000–20,000 |
| **Rental Car Driver** | Inbound tourists about to rent a car | *"I'm renting a car in Japan next week."* | ¥10,000–15,000 |

Each plays a different role:

- **Paper Driver — stable cash flow.** Well-understood need, existing competitors, predictable demand.
- **Foreign Driver — the margin.** Language plus Japanese road rules plus cross-cultural
  explanation is a genuine premium, and almost nobody supplies it.
- **Rental Car Driver — the growth engine.** See §05.

## 05. Why rental cars are the growth engine

Paper Driver demand is **pull**: someone decides they need help, searches, finds a coach.
That is slow and expensive to acquire.

Rental Car demand can be **placed at the moment of decision**. A tourist booking a car in
Hokkaido through Klook, KKday, Booking or the rental company itself can see, on the payment
page:

> *First time driving in Japan? Take a 90-minute Japan Driving Preparation lesson.*

That is a distribution channel, not an ad. Acquisition cost could be far below search ads,
and the intent is already established — they have just committed to driving in a country
they don't know.

**There is a real safety argument underneath it.** Published analysis puts the accident rate
for foreign tourists on leisure rentals at around **3.0%**, against **0.7%** for Japanese
drivers 【TBD: source not yet confirmed】. That number is why rental companies and insurers have a
reason to say yes.

## 06. Supply — where the coaches come from

The instinct is to recruit professional driving-school instructors. That makes the platform
heavy and expensive before it has demand. Four sources, in order of how early they can be used:

| Source | Why | Pay / 2h |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Retired or part-time school instructors** | They know how to *teach*, not just how to drive | ¥5,000–8,000 |
| **Experienced everyday drivers** | The largest supply pool: 10+ years driving, no major accidents, willing to work weekends | — |
| **Foreign residents who drive here** | Chinese → Chinese, Korean → Korean, English → English. The differentiator nobody else has | — |
| **Rental companies, driving schools, hotels** | They already have cars, space and instructors. We bring customers, multilingual support and the order system | Revenue share |

That last row matters most: **the platform never needs to own a car.**

Three coach types follow from this:

| Type | For | Price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 🟢 **Certified Instructor** | Paper Driver | ¥15,000–20,000 |
| 🔵 **Driving Buddy** — vetted experienced driver | Foreign Driver | ¥12,000–18,000 |
| 🟠 **Japan Driving Guide** — bilingual | Tourists | ¥12,000–20,000 |

## 07. Product

Three functions:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Coach onboarding** | Licence upload is mandatory; every coach is manually vetted before the profile goes live |
| **Search & match** | Filter by prefecture/city, transmission (AT / MT / EV), speciality (expressway, parallel parking, night driving, test prep, road-trip prep, city driving) and hourly rate |
| **Community** | Driving tips, road-trip write-ups, used-car buying experiences — the reason to come back after the lessons end |

**Why vetting is not optional.** The entire premise is that a nervous learner feels safe.
A C2C marketplace where anyone can list themselves would destroy the one thing the product
is selling. Manual review is slow and doesn't scale — and it is the correct trade at this
stage.

**Why the community exists.** Lessons end. Without a reason to return, every user is a
one-off transaction. The community is a retention bet, not a feature request.

## 08. Revenue model — and the constraint that shaped it

Learners pay through the platform. DriveMate takes **20%**; 80% goes to the coach via
Stripe Connect.

### The regulatory constraint

Holding money on behalf of a third party in Japan is a **funds-transfer business
(資金移動業)** and requires an FSA licence. For a solo founder, that is not a realistic
path.

So the payment flow was designed around the exemptions in the revised Payment Services Act:

1. **Platform-operator exemption** — the platform is an indispensable part of how the
   contract between learner and coach comes into existence, then collects and forwards
   payment under that contract.
2. **Escrow-type exemption** — funds are held only until the service is delivered, then
   released. Explicitly carved out for platform and sharing-economy services.

The booking flow follows from that, not from UX preference:

| Event | What happens to the money |
| --- | --- |
| Booking | Learner pays immediately; funds sit in the platform balance |
| Coach accepts | Pure acceptance — no money movement |
| Coach cancels (any time) | Full refund to learner |
| Learner cancels > 24h before | Full refund |
| Learner cancels < 24h before | Cancel button locked; dispute flow only |
| Service delivered | Confirm receipt / raise a dispute / auto-confirm after 48h |
| Dispute | Admin refunds or releases to coach |

> The 24-hour lock and the 48-hour auto-confirm are not UX decisions. They are what keeps
> the flow inside the escrow exemption while stopping a learner from consuming a coach's
> time and then walking away.

## 11. The honest part

**I built the platform before validating the demand.**

The riskiest assumption — that this preference converts into payment — is the one thing
the code cannot answer. Ten coaches, an Instagram account and a LINE ID could have matched
the first hundred bookings **by hand**, and would have answered it in a few weeks. Instead
there is a full platform with escrow, dispute resolution and three-language support,
serving nobody.

The market model in §4 has the same shape of error waiting in it: it is arithmetic on
assumptions. ¥5.18bn is not a finding. **Twenty people paying ¥15,000 would be.**

The build was not wasted: it exists, it works, and it is ready the moment demand shows up.
But the order was wrong, and the plan's own Phase 1 is effectively a correction — go back
and do the cheap test that should have come first.
